**1. Choose the variables.** This page is the one place to choose what is searched; the Build page shows, under each condition, which of its values are. **Show** narrows the list to one part of the strategy. Add each value you want searched: a feature's setting (the EMA's period, or a moving average's type), a number in a rule (RSI below 30), a rule's comparison (crosses above or crosses below), a risk dial (the stop distance, the take profit, the bars a trade is held, the size and what a share of the account is of; whether there is a stop and which kind, whether there is a take profit, and how each is measured: in points, in % of the price, as a multiple of a line, or at a line's level), a portfolio setting, or which version of a rule to use.

A comparison swaps only with those that read the same sides: above, below, at or above, at or below and the two crosses with each other, and rising with falling (between swaps with nothing). Letting the optimiser pick the direction is choosing the idea after seeing the data, so judge it on held-back history or walk-forward.

For each, give **a range** (start, step, stop) or **a list of values**. The grid tries every step; the other searchers pick any value in the range, whole numbers for a whole-number setting. The count under each, and the total, show how big a full grid is.

When the risk and a stop in % of the price are searched together, the page says how much of the search asks for trades worth more than the leverage allows. A trade risking r over a stop of s is worth r ÷ s times the account (0.20 over 0.04 is 5x). Where that is above the leverage, each entry is cut to the leverage, so there the risk makes no difference, or skipped, when a trade too big to fund is skipped. Searching a share of the account per trade, up to the leverage, beside the stop covers the same trades with every one funded.

Each part of this page is a section that folds by its title, so a long list of variables can be put away.

**2. Choose how to search.** One search, walk-forward, or a comparison of searches. Every backend is asked the same two things: the **backend** and its **method**. Optuna (TPE, CMA-ES, NSGA-II, random, quasi-random) is the usual choice; BUTChC and Optuna can also search between versions of a rule. Grid search runs one trial per backtest, or many per backtest for settings that change only the signals. SciPy offers differential evolution, dual annealing and DIRECT; Nevergrad offers particle swarm, CMA-ES, differential evolution, (1+1) and TBPSA, in batches on the worker processes. A backend with one method (random search, BUTChC, marsopt) shows it, fixed. A comparison races the backends and methods you pick, each several times. Every setting on this page is kept while the Studio stays open, so you can leave the page and come back to them.

**3. Set the objective and the guard rails.** The metric to maximise or minimise, a minimum number of trades (without one, optimisers find settings that trade twice and win both), and optional drawdown and profit-factor limits. A trial that breaks one counts as invalid, and the search is told how far from valid it was (28 trades of 30 is nearer than 4), so it learns to stay out of where trials are invalid. A trial whose entry signals are fewer than the minimum trades cannot reach it, as every trade opens on one: it is ruled out from its signals, with no backtest, which takes a fraction of the time. It still counts against the budget, and the job's card and its results say how many were.

**4. Hold some history back, and score across folds.** The optimiser never sees the held-back part; the best result is then run on it once, from the starting cash, as each search trial is. Walk-forward does this repeatedly.

**Score each trial across folds** cuts the searched history into back-to-back folds and runs each trial on every one, from the starting cash, then combines their scores into the trial's score. Settings that worked in one stretch of the history and not the others then score lower. Before they are combined, each fold's return counts as its growth a year (its log), so a fold that halved weighs as much as one that doubled, and a profit factor counts as its log; a fold with no trades scores as no edge.

- **Average less one spread** (the default): the folds' average less their standard deviation, the score of a fairly bad period. It rewards settings that do well in every fold over settings that do very well in some and badly in others. The plain average adds little to one run of the whole history: back-to-back folds compound to it.
- **Worst fold**: the strictest; it rests on one fold. **Median fold**: not moved by one very good or one disastrous fold. **Average**: for comparison.
- **Folds that must be profitable**: consistency as a rule; a trial with fewer folds that made money is invalid.

The minimum trades applies to the folds' total. With few trades in each fold, a fold's score is mostly chance: the range check says how many trades each fold makes at the strategy's own settings, and warns under about 10. An even number of folds also gives the probability of backtest overfitting on the results page. Every fold of a trial runs in one backtest call. Not with walk-forward (its windows are folds already) or many trials per backtest.

**5. Start.** **Start optimisation** first checks the ranges, unless they were just checked for these settings: it runs the strategy at its own settings, then each variable at the start and at the end of its range with the others at their own, on the history the search uses. An end that makes fewer trades than the minimum is named, since trials there count as invalid. When the strategy's own settings and every end all fall short, no trial is likely to be valid (a cost typed in the wrong unit, a size under the smallest size), so the job is not started: the page says what the backtests said, and **Start anyway** starts it regardless. **Check the ranges** runs the same check without starting. Each point's result is kept, so a check of the same strategy on the same data, after leaving the page or a restart, runs only the points it has not run before.

Jobs run in the background, with no window of their own, and keep going if you close the page. Under **Jobs**, each running job shows what it is doing, a bar of trials done (and how many were ruled out from their signals), the best so far, how long it has run and about how long is left. The first trials take longest, while each worker process loads the backtest engine; more worker processes run more trials at once but each needs its own memory. A job whose process ended from outside (the computer restarted, or ran out of memory) is marked **stopped**, with what it had done, until you start another job; then its row says it stopped. Each finished job is a row: its number (**#**, the same on the Optimiser results page), its name, the backend and method it searched with, the objective, the best score, and its trials, with how it ended when that was not done (cancelled, failed, stopped). Most searches run a batch of trials side by side and wait for the slowest before choosing the next, so a batch smaller than the worker processes leaves some idle; dual annealing and mixed adaptive random search (marsopt) run one trial at a time. A tick under **Saved** marks a job saved on the Optimiser results page. **Delete the jobs not saved** deletes every other finished job, with its trials, backtest and log, once you confirm; running jobs are never deleted. Results are on the **Optimiser results** page, where **Apply to the strategy** writes the best values back, and where a job is saved, renamed or deleted on its own.
